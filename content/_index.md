Some of my favorite childhood memories are of playing board games, though I'll admit my win rate for many years was subpar. I give credit where it's due: my dad is remarkably smart and somehow always knew how to beat me. But most of my losses came not at the behest of my opponent, but from my own experimenting. Whether the game was *Monopoly*, *Sorry!* or the *Game of Life*, I refused to play the "normal" way (and I still do). My head was always full of odd strategies I wanted to test on the board. Trying them would earn a puzzled look from my dad, followed soon after by a losing position. I learned early that I was not the Bobby Fischer of *Monopoly*. But on the days where some wacky strategy I thought of came to fruition and I tasted victory, no satisfaction came close and I chased that feeling every game after.

Studying mathematics at Middlebury and then completing my M.A. in Statistics at Columbia, I came to understand and appreciate the machinery that underlies these board games: probability, optimization, etc. My old compulsions to try strange strategies no longer were limited to trial-and-error with friends; I am equipped to test them mathematically and determine which ones actually work with evidence. What started as a childhood recreation became something I plan to study for a lifetime.

All of my research is about how many decision-makers interact, similar to players on a board game — just different scopes. With Professor Sharon Di, I worked on [coordinating autonomous vehicles in traffic shared with human drivers](/research/alpha-potential-traffic/), to improve how cities flow. With Shayan Ravari, I used divergence theory to study [the tradeoff in poker between exploiting an opponent and staying safe from being exploited](/research/phi-divergence-leduc/). And with Professor Phil Chodrow, I worked on [degree assortativity in combined networks](/research/network-assortativity/).

Right now, my attention is on the board game [*Century*](/projects/century-golem/). I've built a working online multiplayer version of the game, along with a simulated environment where I can (a) tune bots that give a solo player a fair game, using machine learning to determine which features matter most for winning, and (b) find the optimal way to play, i.e. a win in the fewest possible turns. There are many other subtopics in *Century* that interest me and I plan to cover them elsewhere on this site.

Outside of research, I love basketball, fantasy football (not looking good this season), and classic *World of Warcraft*.
